# Objection to Internal Ask

Most stalled deals are not stalled on persuasion. They are stalled because a buyer asked something, a rep forwarded it inward, and the internal recipient could not tell what was being asked of them, so it went to the bottom of the pile.

This skill rewrites the forward. Input is what the buyer said. Output is a message an engineer, a lawyer, or a founder can act on in under two minutes.

## Step 1: Separate the ask from the noise

Buyer messages mix several things. Pull them apart before writing anything:

- **The actual request.** Often one sentence inside four paragraphs.
- **The stated reason.** What the buyer says is driving it.
- **The real driver, where it is inferable.** A procurement checklist, a policy they must comply with, a competing vendor's claim, an internal reviewer with a veto. Mark this as inference, never as fact.
- **The consequence of no.** Does the deal die, shrink, slip, or continue unchanged? This is what determines urgency, and it is the thing most forwards omit.
- **The real deadline**, separated from the artificial one.

If the buyer's message is genuinely ambiguous about what is being asked, say so and draft the clarifying question to the buyer instead. Do not manufacture an internal ask from a guess. An internal team burned by a mis-specified ask is slower on the next one.

## Step 2: Convert the ask into a decision

Internal people do not respond well to problems. They respond to decisions with options attached.

Turn the request into a specific decision with two or three concrete options, each with a cost. Options are things like: say yes as asked, say yes with a condition, offer an alternative that meets the underlying need, say no and hold. Where an option has been used before on another deal, say so, because precedent is the single most persuasive input to an internal approver.

## Step 3: Name one owner

One name, not a team, and not two names. Where genuinely unclear, name the most likely owner and say the assumption out loud so it gets corrected fast. A message addressed to everyone is answered by no one.

If the user supplied an org chart or team list, use it. If not, infer from the subject matter and mark the inference.

## Step 4: Output

```markdown
**Ask:** [One sentence. What decision is needed from whom]
**Owner:** [Name]
**Needed by:** [Date, and what happens if it slips]

**Context:** [Two or three lines. The deal, the value, the stage, what the buyer said]

**Options**
1. [Option]. [Cost, effort, precedent if any]
2. [Option]. [Cost, effort, precedent if any]
3. [Option]. [Cost, effort, precedent if any]

**Recommendation:** [Which option, and the one-line reason]

**If we do nothing:** [The concrete consequence, with a date]
```

Keep the whole thing under 200 words, with each option on one line. The limit is the point: a long internal ask is a deferred internal ask. If three options will not fit, cut to two, because a third option is usually the weakest one anyway.

## Step 5: Offer the buyer-facing half

After the internal message, offer to draft the holding reply to the buyer, since a buyer waiting in silence assumes the answer is no. A good holding reply confirms the request in the buyer's own words, names a date for a real answer, and asks nothing. Draft it only if the user wants it, but always offer.

## Rules

- Preserve the buyer's own wording for the request itself. Rephrasing a requirement is how a company ends up solving the wrong problem for two weeks.
- Never inflate urgency. If the close date is soft, say it is soft. Internal teams calibrate on this, and inflated urgency spends credibility that the genuinely urgent ask will need.
- Separate what the buyer requires from what the buyer would prefer. Requirements block deals. Preferences do not, and treating a preference as a requirement is a common and expensive error.
- Where a request has come up on more than one deal, say so. Recurring requests justify a permanent fix, one-offs do not.
- Do not commit the company to anything in the draft. This message asks for a decision, it does not announce one.
