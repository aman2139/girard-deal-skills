# Escalation Drafter

An escalation that reads as a question gets answered when convenient. An escalation that reads as a dated decision gets answered by the date. The difference is entirely in how it is written, and this skill writes it.

Use this when a decision must be made by someone with authority the requester does not have. Where the ask is really a coordination request rather than an approval, use `objection-to-internal-ask` instead.

## Step 1: Get the four facts

Do not draft without these. Ask in one message if they are missing:

1. **The exception being requested.** The specific deviation from standard, in numbers where numbers exist.
2. **The deal.** Value, stage, close date, and whether the date is real or aspirational.
3. **The approver.** One name.
4. **The alternative.** What happens if the answer is no. Not the worst case, the likely case.

Where the requester cannot state the alternative, that is diagnostic: the escalation may not be needed at all. Say so before drafting. Escalations that did not need to happen are what make approvers slow on the ones that do.

## Step 2: Frame the decision honestly

Three failure modes to avoid, in order of frequency:

- **Foregone conclusion.** An escalation written so only one answer is possible reads as manipulation and gets a slower answer, not a faster one. Give the approver a real no.
- **Inflated urgency.** A fake deadline works once. After that every deadline from that requester is discounted, including the real ones.
- **Buried ask.** The decision appears in paragraph four. The approver reads paragraph one.

State the ask in the first line. Always.

## Step 3: Price the delay

The cost of waiting is the piece almost every escalation omits, and it is what actually moves an approver. Convert delay into something concrete: a slipped quarter, a renewal date missed, a champion who loses internal credibility, a procurement window that closes and reopens next quarter.

Where the true cost of delay is small, say that too. An honest "this can wait two weeks, but not four" earns the credibility that the next urgent escalation will spend.

## Step 4: Output

```markdown
**Subject:** Decision needed by [date]: [the exception] on [account]

[Name], asking for a decision on [the specific exception] for [account].

**Deal:** [value] · [stage] · [close date] · [term length]
**Standard position:** [what we normally do]
**Requested:** [the deviation, in numbers]
**Precedent:** [prior comparable decision, or "none found"]

**If yes:** [what it costs, what it unlocks]
**If no:** [what likely happens, honestly stated]
**If no answer by [date]:** [the concrete consequence]

**My recommendation:** [one line]
```

Under 150 words in the body. Attachments and detail go below the signature or in a linked doc, never inside the ask.

## Step 5: Handle the follow-up case

When this is a second or third attempt at the same escalation, do not resend the same message with "bumping this" on top. Rewrite it:

- Lead with what changed since the last ask, because nothing changing is itself a fact worth stating
- Restate the deadline, and note how much of the runway is now gone
- Narrow the ask if possible. A blocked decision often unblocks when it is cut into a smaller one
- Offer a default: "if I do not hear back by Thursday I will tell them no and hold the standard position." A default is what converts a stalled approval into a decision, because silence now has a consequence the approver may not want.

## Rules

- One decision per escalation. Two decisions in one message reliably produce zero.
- Numbers, not adjectives. "22% discount on a 2-year prepay" beats "a significant discount".
- Include the precedent search result even when it is empty. "No comparable prior approval found" tells the approver they are setting policy, not just closing a deal, and that changes how carefully they answer.
- Never characterise the approver's likely answer inside the message.
- Do not attach the whole email thread. Summarise it in two lines and offer the thread on request.
- Match the company's register. A four-person startup does not need a memo, and a message that reads as corporate ceremony in a small team gets read as a complaint.
